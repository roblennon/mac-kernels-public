This is the notebook for thoughts that are not experiments yet. Forty-four thinking lenses across three idea rounds each wrote down how they thought, what they doubted, and what they gave up on. Four reader agents distilled those accounts into the entries below, and a fifth read the whole of it as a sceptical colleague. The queue of experiments lives in [QUEUE.md](QUEUE.md) and every individual idea is in [the idea ledger](../2026-11-09-idea-ledger.md). This file keeps the reasoning that sits around them.

Two cautions for a reader. Every statement about a paper here is an agent's report of what it read, often from an abstract, often during throttled searches, and has not been checked again. And the entries keep the lenses' own reasoning without judging it: some of it is surely wrong, which is part of why it is here and not in the queue.

The sections run from the widest view to the narrowest: the critic's reading of the whole set, then hidden theories, wild thoughts, reframings, ways of thinking that produced ideas, and warnings. After those come the beliefs the lenses questioned, the open questions worth carrying, and the ideas that died, with how they died. ## A sceptical reader on the whole notebook

Written by a reviewing agent after reading every lens account. Its headings are kept as they came.

### One writer, forty-four masks

The notebook reads as forty-four lenses, but the voice does not change. Each lens takes an old, exact theorem from somewhere else, such as Haldane's balance, Kramers' escape law, von Neumann's restoring organs, Stein's lemma, Jackson's limit cycles or the Clarke and Barron result. It then lines the theorem up against one published number and reports that the number fits. The cross-pollination lens nearly says this itself: 71 ideas turn out to rest on about five hidden quantities. The same holds across the whole set. Von Neumann's 1956 theorem shows up in at least three lenses that never cite each other. "Sampling below temperature one starves rare events" shows up in five. The 330 ideas are far less independent than the count suggests. A portfolio built from them would be one bet repeated many times.

The shared taste is easy to name. A good result is a cheap measurement that settles a dispute between two papers, exposes an artefact, or turns a vague claim into a ratio with no free parameters. The writer loves the one-minute calculation, the matched random control, and the design where "either answer is publishable". This is a good taste, but it is narrow. It is the taste of a referee, not of an inventor. Almost every idea is defined by a gap between other people's papers, so its value depends on those papers staying important.

### What counts as a finding here

Three assumptions run through the whole notebook without being stated.

The first is that a finding means explaining something. Laws, thresholds, exponents and debunkings count. A thing people use does not. Only the build-something-new lens asks what should exist, and its answer was a benchmark, which is still an instrument for judging others.

The second is that novelty means not having found it in a search. Nearly every lens reports that arXiv and Semantic Scholar rate-limited it, that it read abstracts only, and that "not found" is weak. That warning is honest. But it also shaped the territory. Ideas were steered toward gaps the searches happened to show, and the contradictions the notebook works hardest on are often between two abstracts.

The third is that the right subject is a mechanism in the model, studied as an accident of training. People appear in the notebook mostly as sources of folklore, and adversaries hardly appear at all.

### What other traditions would say

A statistician would like the move in the agents lens. There, heterogeneity across tasks explains a falling Weibull hazard with no within-run change at all. The statistician would then point out that the same discipline is missing almost everywhere else. The spin-glass lens matches a predicted 0.18 to a measured 0.2 from one model. The speech lens matches a turn-taking median to within the paper's range and admits it distrusts the match. Neither one is evidence yet. With 330 hypotheses drawn from the same handful of public models, a few close numerical matches are expected by chance. The human-in-loop lens is the only one that proposes committing predictions by hash before looking at the data, and that standard should apply to everything.

A systems builder would ask why round two banned quantization and kernels as a subject. That is the one area where this researcher has working code, a measured result, and puzzles nobody else holds, such as the N times K squared penalty and the error floor under rotation. The robs-instruments lens is the only one built on owned data: Kira's labels, Imaginator's kept-versus-discarded log, the tic catalogue, and the codebook-order accident in the voice lab. Data nobody else holds is the most defensible advantage in the whole notebook, and it gets the least weight.

A cognitive scientist would see that the human-in-loop lens is close to alone. It proposes that "machine feel" is too little variation at long time scales and that a listener learns the generator over time. Everywhere else, quality means a metric, and nobody asks what a person perceives.

A musician would agree and go further. The notebook treats time as a grid that causes aliasing. A musician treats timing as expression, with groove, rubato and the long arc of a performance. The tempo-quantization idea of 3000/k beats per minute is the only place music appears, and it is framed as a defect.

A hardware designer would like the noise-margin framing and the claim that energy depends on the values in the matrices. They would then ask why one M1 Max is treated as representative of anything beyond itself.

A security researcher would find the notebook strangely innocent. Mismatched chat templates, the sampler order, a 26-byte update that adds 29 points, and subliminal transfer are all treated as curiosities. A security researcher would treat each of them as an attack surface. The prompt-injection idea was dropped because ARGUS existed, and nothing replaced it.

An economist would ask who pays for each answer and what changes once it is known. The lenses rank ideas by how cheap they are to kill, not by how much they are worth if true. The no-boss lens notes that the speedrun is now crowded with AI-assisted entrants. That is a market signal the rest of the notebook ignores.

### The boldest thoughts

Four thoughts are actually bold, meaning that if they hold, someone senior has to change their mind.

The first is the neuro-bio lens's claim that AdamW turns Haldane's graded balance into Eigen's error threshold. Under that claim, rare features below a critical frequency are not learned at all, and beta2 sets that frequency. It puts an optimizer constant into a statement about what a model can know.

The second is the agents lens's reading of task-horizon curves. On that reading, the comforting falling hazard is an artefact of mixing easy and hard tasks, and the true within-run hazard may rise.

The third is the cluster of ideas around sharpened sampling of rare decisions. It covers urn monopoly at temperature 0.7, late turn-taking, stopping and refusing, and loops caused by fixed errors. It claims that one decoding interaction explains failures that text, speech and agent researchers each file as their own problem.

The fourth is the data-and-tokens lens's "fossil record". The idea is that a trained model's outputs reveal its preprocessing pipeline, and so reveal compute spent on detail that never existed in the training data.

### Thoughts that look bold but are safe

Several ideas sound daring but carry little risk and little reward. The Kramers escape law for softmax is exact by construction, so the only open question is the size of the effect. The min-p and temperature-order identity is arithmetic, and it is useful hygiene rather than science. The mel-hop beat at 106 Hz is defended in advance on the grounds that "pays nothing" would itself be citable. In practice it would be cited by nobody. The contradictions lens's promise that "one table joins them" is careful work that leaves both original papers standing. The orthogonality test for guidance weight is the same kind of thing: an identity that holds for any model, measured once. Each of these is worth an afternoon. None of them can carry a year.

### The year-long question, and the case against it

Read as a whole, the notebook keeps returning to one question. When a generative model reads its own output over a long horizon, how much of the decline comes from the decoding loop rather than the model? The loop includes temperature, errors that repeat on every step, the moment the prompt leaves a finite window, and the transcript as evidence about its own author. The agents lens, the tiny-model lens, the folklore lens, the speech lens, the dynamical-systems lens and old-tricks all reach it. It has an exact null: for a Bayesian learner, drift is a martingale with bounded variance. It can be tested in toy worlds where the right answer is known, which suits a Mac. It also points to fixes that cost nothing at inference time.

The case against it is strong. Long-horizon failure is where well-funded labs are concentrated, and it is the regime where one machine is slowest, because it needs long generations and many seeds. The supporting evidence so far is urn simulations and one suspiciously good match. A trained transformer may not behave like the urn at all. The likeliest outcome is "partly decoding, partly model", which is hard to publish. Choosing the unifying question also indulges the notebook's own bias toward one grand mechanism. A less glamorous year on the researcher's own instruments and owned data might produce more that nobody else can replicate.

## Hidden theories

### The speedrun and the slowrun pull small models in opposite directions

*Lens: arch*

The architecture lens read two public small-scale benchmarks side by side and found that they reward opposite designs. The modded-nanoGPT speedrun uses fresh data and a time limit. Its latest record gained the most from a sampled-softmax loss and from a hashed n-gram lookup table with 84.6M rows and 65B parameters, and the speedrun has generally grown lookup tables and cut depth. The table's author says gains are roughly logarithmic in table size and the table is already too large to scale to frontier training. The NanoGPT Slowrun fixes the data at 100M FineWeb tokens and lets compute run free. There, the winners removed the value-embedding tables, looped layers late in training, and added regularization such as stochastic depth, weight averaging and heavy weight decay; the unlimited track is all ensembles and chain distillation. The lens's point is that nobody has written down the variable that decides which approach wins. A plausible candidate is how much fresh data each parameter sees, but the lens leaves that as the open question that drives its 'loop or look up' idea. It also notes that Engram suppresses its table only at inference, abruptly, and never trains with a table and then removes it. The lens ranked the 'loop or look up' study as the most likely to give a publishable finding either way.

### A single number is being trusted to stand for a state

*Lens: backwards*

This lens wrote down thirty common assumptions and asked, for each, what the world would look like if the opposite held. Most died. What survived shared one hidden belief: a single number can stand for the state of a model. The clearest case is validation loss in the modded-nanoGPT speedrun, where "3.28 on FineWeb" is treated as one model quality. The lens reports reading three open pull requests from September and October. In those, records now come from exact n-gram lookups over 37 billion tokens, count tables mixed into the output, and a document cache. A commenter there already calls it a case study in gaming. So "equal loss means equal model" is visibly false at the extreme. The open question is whether it was already false across two years of earlier records. Nobody would have noticed, because nobody measures anything but the one number. The same belief has a training-time form. A model that starts at a given loss is assumed to be as far along as one that trained down to that loss. If that is false, head starts from closed-form statistics would fade during training. People would report the early gain and never check the end. The lens calls this a long shot it would hesitate to say aloud, but notes it is cheap to kill.

### The falling hazard in task-horizon fits may be an artefact of mixing tasks

*Lens: capabilities-agents-and-long-horizon-behaviour*

Ord and Hamilton fit a Weibull curve to pass or fail by task length and get a shape below one, about 0.6 to 0.9 for models and 0.37 for humans. They read this as a hazard that falls as a task goes on. But Sinha et al. (2509.09677) measure inside a run and find per-step accuracy gets worse as errors pile up. Any survival analyst would note that a mix of easy and hard tasks produces a falling population hazard even when every run has a constant or rising hazard. The lens checked this: a constant hazard per task with a log-normal spread across tasks of 1.5 to 2.0 log units gives a fitted shape of 0.64 to 0.49, the published range, with no within-run decline at all. So the favourable reading may be an artefact. A second calculation shows why the true effect hides. A model where each visible error raises the next step's error odds looks like constant hazard at 20 steps but is catastrophic at 200, with 77 percent of runs above a 20 percent error rate. Removing errors from view at 0.2 per step returns it to constant hazard. That is a harness intervention, not a model one.

### On Apple GPUs the decode pass is already paid, so fixed-rate formats are a CUDA habit

*Lens: compression*

The compression lens reads the repo's most valuable fact as this: on M1 to M4 GPUs the multiply is fp16 whatever the stored format, so decoding the weights is already paid for, and the fixed-rate tile rule that shapes every CUDA format does not apply. By its own arithmetic from the repo's weight-error table, not from a source, the shipped formats sit about 0.7 to 1.2 bits per weight above the Gaussian Shannon bound. Two papers close that gap on paper, WaterSIC and HyperQuant, but both stop before a measured fused kernel, and an August 2026 transforms survey names that as an open problem. The physics lens adds that the entropy-coding papers of 2026 all stop at server tensor-core GPUs, batch throughput, or no kernel at all. The compression lens questioned several more assumptions: that every checkpoint in a family must be coded on its own, that diffusion quality can only be measured by sampling, that rotation and low-rank fixes stack, that forward and backward need the same weight bits, and that memory pressure has to mean swap. It corrected one earlier lead: the sign-symmetry fine-tuning paper is withdrawn as false, so the low-bit backward idea rests on no prior support.

### Fixed grids chosen by habit, read with the sampling theorem

*Lens: control-signal*

Several places in neural networks use a discrete grid chosen once by habit: the 10 ms log-mel hop from Kaldi, RoPE's fastest band at 1 radian per token from the 2017 sinusoidal table, and 2x2 DiT patches written by four separate sets of output weights. For RoPE, positions are integers, so the highest informative frequency is pi radians per token, leaving the top 1.65 octaves empty. A linear programme for the best margin a head can get on one exact offset gave about 0.3 of the budget with standard bands, nearly unchanged from head width 64 to 128, and about 0.6 with three bands between 1.7 and 2.9 radians. The fastest band alone sets the limit. For the mel front end, a 25 ms window does not resolve harmonics near 100 Hz, so low channels beat at the pitch, and the 100 Hz hop folds a 106 Hz beat into a 6 Hz wobble at the syllable rate. A numpy check found several dB of wobble at 106 to 110 Hz and 190 to 197 Hz and none at 120 Hz. The lens kept only cases where the fixed, unlearned part does the sampling, because there the linear theory binds whatever the network learned.

### Quantization error in a sampler may be a repeated bias, not noise

*Lens: crossmodal*

Three round-one lenses, crossmodal, compression and outsider, questioned the same assumption: that quantization error in a diffusion sampler behaves like noise. The crossmodal lens suggests it may be a per-sample bias that repeats at every step, which changes what one should do about it. The compression lens asks the converse, whether a time-integrating sampler could average out the error rather than having it corrected as a fixed thing. The outsider lens frames quantization loss as partly a calibration shift rather than unstructured noise. The crossmodal lens also found that the obvious transfers between fields were already done within the last year, and that the open gaps sit where two communities do not read each other: speech-token language models versus diffusion caching, interpretability tools versus the quantization formats people actually download, and video-codec practice such as I-frames, delta frames and rate-distortion curves versus quantization benchmarks. It adds that decode cost measured at LLM batch 1 may not apply to diffusion, where thousands of tokens share one decode. One idea, nested-bit extrapolation, it rated low on purpose because its own first-order math predicts failure; it kept it only because the test takes an afternoon and two well-known papers left the question as an aside.

### A trained model is a fossil record of its preprocessing

*Lens: data-and-tokens*

This half-theory says each silent default in a training pipeline leaves a mark in outputs that can be read without the data. JPEG stores colour at half resolution, so a generator trained on web JPEGs may never have seen sharp colour edges, and its outputs should carry 4:2:0 colour bandwidth even when saved as PNG. An edit model trained with one pixel-centre convention and run with another should shift outputs by a fraction of a pixel following a formula in the scale factor. A speech model trained on MP3 or upsampled 16 kHz audio should have an output spectrum that stops where the codec stopped. If right, there is a cheap forensic science: measure the fossil in public samples from many models, infer the pipeline, and ask what compute is wasted on detail that never existed. The killing control is whether the fossil lives in the generator or in the autoencoder before it. A related point: Whisper's log-mel clamp is max minus 8 over whatever array it receives, so the floor depends on whether the whole file, a 30-second chunk, or a streaming buffer is normalised. By the lens's unchecked arithmetic, silence can move by about a quarter of the input range, which matters because Whisper's worst failure is inventing text on silence.

### Curation carries almost no bits, and the failures hold most of them

*Lens: data-as-the-subject*

A keep or discard decision at keep rate r carries at most H(r) bits. Ten thousand decisions at a 10 percent keep rate hold under 4,700 bits, about 600 bytes. A LoRA trained on the kept images has megabytes of capacity, so nearly everything it learns is not the decision but the prompts and the base model's own habits fed back. The lens suspects this is the true shape of model collapse under curation: the curation signal is tiny and the self-distillation signal is huge, so the loop narrows fast and improves slowly. The same arithmetic says which half of an archive matters. At a 10 percent keep rate the keeps hold 71 percent of the bits. At a 90 percent pass rate, typical of verified synthetic data pipelines, the failures hold 71 percent, and those pipelines throw the failures away. The lens could not find this stated but expects someone has written it down. On images, it notes two theories disagree on where a diffusion model needs the most data by noise level, and guesses a hump in the middle, implying many thumbnails plus a few sharp images.

### Token divergence follows a flip law, not chaos

*Lens: dynamical-systems*

A diffusion ODE sampler is a smooth flow whose summed finite-time Lyapunov exponents equal log p(noise) minus log p(image), so it mostly contracts. Token generation is different: output does not change between flips and changes completely at one. A chaotic flow's predictability horizon grows with the log of precision; a flip process's horizon grows linearly. The lens checked this against the tables in the "FP32 or death" paper: bf16 runs diverge at token 44 to 65 and fp16 at 216 to 430, a factor of 5 to 9 for an 8x precision gain. The linear law fits and the log law does not. The authors did not draw this conclusion. Their fp32 runs diverge more than the law predicts, which suggests exact ties or error growing through depth. Separately, softmax sampling is exactly Kramers' escape law with the logit gap as barrier. Reliability engineers test at high temperature to predict failure at room temperature. Convexity says straight-line extrapolation always underestimates low-temperature failure, by 2.3x at T=0.5 in the lens's check, so accelerated testing gives a usable lower bound.

### Rounding error is fixed in time, and fixed error may cause loops

*Lens: folklore*

The lens noticed that several unrelated complaints look like one event: an error that is the same at every step. A rounded weight is wrong in the same way on token 1 and on token 4,000. Fresh noise averages out over a long generation, but a fixed error does not. Pipis and co-authors (read this session) show that reasoning models loop because of errors that are correlated in time. They did not study rounded weights, yet a rounded weight is the most time-correlated error there is. The DRY paper (abstract read) halves loop rates on quantized 70B models but never asks whether the rounding raised the rate. Users say 4-bit models loop and that penalties do not fix it. The proposed experiment separates fixed error from fresh error at equal size. This pulls against an earlier idea that treats rounding as a rise in temperature: higher temperature reduces loops, so if rounding raises them, the temperature picture is incomplete. The same thought extends to speculative decoding, where one user saw acceptance fall from 48% to 33% when a draft head became more precise, and asked whether drafter and target errors should line up. The lens also recalls, without opening any, model cards that recommend a presence penalty for quantized versions; if true, vendors already believe the loop idea and never published the measurement.

### Recursion may do its work during training, not inference

*Lens: future-work*

Several papers circle the question of why iterating a small network on its own output helps. The Tiny Recursive Model paper says outright it has no theory for it. Attractor Models note that the model learns to reach its fixed point in fewer steps as training goes on, and leave it for later. A follow-up note on the ARC checkpoint found most of the score arrives at the first recursion step. A web search summary says the ARC Prize team found, for the earlier HRM model, that repeated supervision mattered more than the hierarchy. Put together, the lens reads these as suggesting recursion does its work during training, not during inference. Nobody it found has stated or tested that directly. Separately, the lens states a law it did not find written anywhere for continuous diffusion language models: a token should become decided when the signal-to-noise ratio pays for its surprisal. On a toy of 3,000 random unit vectors, the true token's posterior crossed one half at noise variance 1/(2 x surprisal) for surprisals of 2, 4 and 6 nats. The transition was wider than the textbook formula, so the law would be about the midpoint, not the shape. It notes an unread appendix (2610.13834 Appendix G) may already state it.

### Temperature has no shared definition for continuous-token autoregressive models

*Lens: inference-algos*

The inference-algorithms lens found LLM decoding crowded, with nearly every text idea published in the past year. The young area is sampling for autoregressive models that emit continuous tokens through a small diffusion, flow or consistency head, such as MAR, NextStep-1, VibeVoice, DiTAR and Kyutai's CALM. In these models each paper defines 'temperature' differently and none derives it. MAR scales the sampler noise. Kyutai shrinks the Gaussian variance, to 0.8 for speech. DiTAR picks the time point where noise enters the reverse ODE. GIVT calls it variance scaling. Temperature and guidance are swept per model against FID or word error rate. Nucleus sampling has no continuous counterpart in use. Several papers fight error accumulation by injecting noise into the context during training, but none separates what the model emits from what it feeds back to itself at inference. Four of the lens's five ideas sit in that gap. The lens adds a practical reason this suits a one-person lab: the head is tiny next to the backbone, so per-token Monte Carlo, exact densities and noise inversion are cheap, and the work can run on an M1 Max and a free T4.

### Measure the squared-error-optimal guidance weight

*Lens: information-geometry*

A regression trained with squared error converges to the conditional mean, and the residual of a conditional mean is uncorrelated with every function of the input. A diffusion denoiser is such a regression. So for any trained denoiser there are exact identities needing no sampling: the best scalar gain on its output is 1, and the best mix of conditional and unconditional predictions for predicting the true target puts weight exactly 1 on the conditional one. Classifier-free guidance uses weights of 3 to 7, and everyone agrees guidance is 'not about likelihood'. But the lens could not find anyone who measured the squared-error-optimal weight on held-out captioned images, noise level by noise level. If it is above 1 at middle noise levels, part of guidance repairs a model that under-uses its caption, and the repair should be larger with better captions. If it is 1.00 everywhere, guidance is purely sharpening and the under-fit story from the autoguidance paper does not apply to text conditioning. Either result is worth a short paper and costs a few hundred forward passes. A related half-theory: under Adam, late-training jitter is about the learning rate in every coordinate, while a Bayesian posterior holds sensitive weights tightly; the per-weight mismatch might map under- and over-trained directions, but the lens saw no clean number yet.

### Every update is a small signal part plus a rule-dependent diffusion part

*Lens: learning-rules-beyond-pretraining*

The lens found much of post-training claimed in the last year, but sees laws missing: papers report a pattern and stop before the exponent. It proposes that five results are views of one split. RL forgets less than SFT. RL needs 13 parameters where SFT needs 100 to 1000 times more. Evolution strategies move every weight a lot yet only a sparse part matters and little is forgotten. LoRA merges fail through a few shared output directions. Adam is reported to forget more than SGD. Any update is a signal part, in the few directions the objective constrains, plus a diffusion part the learning rule moves for unrelated reasons. A reasoning fine-tune's signal is tens of dimensions. SFT on someone else's text has a large diffusion part because matching another writer's surface form constrains thousands of irrelevant directions. Adam has a large one because it gives every coordinate with nonzero gradient a similar step, including pure noise. Full-parameter ES is an isotropic random walk and survives because language models tolerate isotropic weight noise far better than expected, the same tolerance that makes 4-bit weights work. On-policy RL with a tiny adapter has almost none. If right, forgetting, merge interference, the ES step budget and perhaps loss of plasticity could be predicted from how much a rule diffuses and how much noise the model tolerates. The lens lacks an operational definition of constrained directions, and notes noise tolerance is not actually isotropic.

### Distance from the reference output is treated as quality lost, with no noise floor

*Lens: measurement*

The measurement lens found that the field treats distance from the full-precision or full-step output as quality lost, but nobody publishes the noise floor of that distance. It cites an Ideogram paper in which INT8 scores an LPIPS of 0.243 against FP8 while preference metrics cannot tell the two apart. The inversion lens reached the same point from another side: same-seed fidelity is treated as quality loss, and nobody subtracts a matched-noise control. The measurement lens names a second pattern, shared lineage between the judge and the judged. Caching methods are scored against the very trajectory they copy, and speech models trained on Whisper transcripts are scored by Whisper. The third pattern is cheap proxies that are assumed to keep rankings, such as calibration error, package power, or tokens per second on one GPU class, and which fail each time someone checks. The assumptions the lens questioned are that a paired metric measures damage, that one seed is enough, that the default-step output is ground truth, that clean-speech WER is evaluator-neutral, that fp16 means the same arithmetic on every GPU, and that a speedup measured on an H100 ranks methods the same way on a Mac.

### Weight decay under AdamW turns a graded balance into a cliff

*Lens: neuro-bio*

The lens argues only biology's balance laws transfer to networks, not its mechanism descriptions, because SGD with weight decay is literally a degrading and a restoring force acting on the same numbers for a long time. Haldane's 1937 result says a harmful allele sits at mutation rate over selection strength, and the fitness lost equals the mutation rate regardless of harm size. The same algebra for a feature of frequency f under weight decay lambda gives residual loss about lambda over f, and a total cost of about lambda times its weight, with f cancelled: every kept feature costs the same. A 15-second numpy toy confirmed this for plain SGD. With AdamW the restoring force stops depending on how wrong the model is, and the graded balance becomes a cliff: features above a critical frequency are fit almost perfectly, those below are not learned. That is Eigen's error threshold. The toy puts the cliff at a frequency proportional to lambda times the square root of one minus beta2, with learning rate cancelling. Pinto, Galanti and Balestriero (2024) derived a lambda-squared threshold from a static model, but the lens found no one treating it as an optimizer-dynamics object with these exponents. A timescale worry follows: the balance takes about 1/(learning rate x lambda) steps, tens to hundreds of thousands, so a short run may show no rare-token damage while a long one shows a cliff, and two papers could disagree for an unnamed reason.

### The additivity premise in layer attribution is checkable exactly

*Lens: no-boss*

Layer importance scores, data attribution, feature attribution and most pruning heuristics assume that removing two things costs about the sum of removing each. Shapley-for-layers papers say exact computation is infeasible and then sample, or restrict to neighbouring layers on the stated premise that distant layers do not interact. Nobody seems to have checked that against the full lattice, though a 16-block model has only 65,536 subsets. The lens worked out that a depth-first walk over the include-or-skip tree needs one block application per subset, because each subset shares a prefix with a sibling. That makes a 16-block lattice about 4,096 full forward passes, an afternoon, and a 24-block lattice about 700,000, a patient week. The RYS result hints that units of function are contiguous runs of about seven layers and that repeating a single layer hurts, so interactions may be strong and local, and greedy block dropping may miss the best set. The half-theory: an interaction length in depth exists and is a property of the training recipe. If it scales as a fixed fraction of depth, a 16-block model is a faithful miniature of an 80-block one. If it is a fixed count of layers, deep models are nearly additive and small-model circuit results overstate how entangled large models are. It needs the lattice at three depths.

### One noise margin per model, and a network that may be digital in what it reads but analog in what it carries

*Lens: old-tricks*

Engineers of scarce machines asked what in a system removes error, and how much per stage. Digital logic beat analog computing because a gate's transfer curve shrinks small noise at every stage, while an analog amplifier only adds it. Von Neumann made this a theorem in 1956: below a per-component error threshold, depth costs nothing; above it, information is lost exponentially. Logic designers turned it into a datasheet number, the noise margin. The lens notes that every approximate-inference trick in ML (caches, low-precision activations, token merging, stale features, fp16 rounding) injects noise into a deep circuit, yet each is measured separately. The old view says there should be one number per model for how much noise is free. Interpretability work (Ferreira da Silva and Heimersheim, June 2026, which the lens read) found residual activations resist small perturbations with a plateau across six models, but read it as evidence about superposition. Nobody the lens found treats it as a noise margin or checks whether it predicts tolerance to real approximations. The lens cannot tell whether a transformer is digital or analog. The residual stream is a sum, where errors add and nothing restores them. Restoration can only come from nonlinear readers: softmax, MLP gates, layer norm. So damage may depend on whether noise lands in a direction a later reader thresholds. Diffusion samplers re-read their output and contract toward the data manifold each step, while few-step distilled models have given up most of that loop.

### Networks have no designed error path

*Lens: old-tricks*

The scarce-machine engineers always designed the error path apart from the signal path: noise shaping, error feedback, parity bits, checksums on matrix products (Huang and Abraham 1984). A neural network has no designed error path. Whatever restoration it has is an accident of training. The lens suggests finding the learned error path, meaning the directions the network treats as not signal. One use is a free alarm, the way a memory controller uses parity. The lens thinks the deeper use is the second one: deliberately dumping approximation error there. A cache or low-precision format could be designed to put its error where the model already ignores it. It could not reduce this to a single prediction and left it as a research direction. A connected question is whether training with dropout or noise injection makes a network measurably more digital, with a wider noise margin at equal loss, which would make the margin a cheap trainable property. Another is Wilkinson's question in weight space: whether an inference shortcut equals the smallest weight change producing the same outputs, and whether that distance is smaller than the distance between two training seeds.

### Search cost follows Renyi-1/2 entropy, not perplexity

*Lens: rate-distortion*

The lens argues ML uses Shannon entropy for jobs where coding theory says a different number governs. Shannon entropy is right with unlimited computation and long blocks. Sequential decoders for convolutional codes have a computational cutoff: above a rate R0 below capacity, expected work is infinite because work per symbol has a Pareto tail. Arikan (1996) showed the cutoff is a Renyi quantity of order one half, the same quantity that sets the growth of Massey's guesswork. A language model driving a tree search is a sequential decoder. So the cost of finding a target by beam search, best-first proof search or constrained decoding should follow the Renyi-1/2 entropy of the conditionals. A numpy check on Zipf-shaped distributions over 50,000 tokens showed a distribution with 2.4 bits of Shannon entropy and 6.3 bits of Renyi-1/2 entropy. If real models look like that, there is a wide band of problems a model knows in the perplexity sense that no feasible search can extract. It would also explain why a small loss improvement sometimes gives a large search-benchmark gain: training shrinks the tail, and the tail is what the Renyi number weights. This was not checked on real logits. The same lens extends this to Slepian-Wolf: a device with no model can compress text by hashing tokens for a model to undo, and that scheme should break at the Renyi rate, not the Shannon rate.

### Base weights as dither: why a requantized LoRA merge still works, and when it should silently fail

*Lens: robs-results*

A requantized LoRA merge in Rob's findings has 2.8% error, about 30 times the LoRA's own contribution, and still renders the person. The lens ran a numpy toy (Gaussian 2048x2048, rank 32, a plain row-max quantizer rather than Rob's format). The difference between the two quantized matrices regressed onto the LoRA with coefficient 1.00 at 8, 6 and 4 bits, even when that difference was 5 to 20 times larger than the LoRA. The base weights act as dither that carries the small update through the rounding. The explanation comes with a threshold. Signal-to-noise inside the LoRA's own subspace was about 6 at 6 bits and about 3 at 4 bits in the toy, so weaker adapters on coarser formats should vanish silently. In the same spirit, the lens found speed numbers following a law nobody asked about. The excess of F.linear's wide-K penalty in fp32 (0.59, 1.52, 7.83 at K of 8192, 16384, 32768) roughly doubles each time K doubles. A cache effect would level off, so this looks like extra work of size N times K squared, for example a strip of the transposed weight rebuilt per strip of the product, which predicts a dependence on M the existing sweep cannot test. It also suggests pointing FPRev at MPS: if the summation tree changes shape at the K where the speed penalty starts, the accuracy puzzle (19 of 66 results matching no simulated order) and the speed puzzle are one puzzle.

### Retrieval failure depends on the variance of distractor logits, not only the gap

*Lens: statistical-physics*

One target key competing with n distractors is the random energy model with one planted low state. The planted state loses when the summed weight of the crowd equals its own. The crowd's weight depends on the variance of its logits, not only on the gap. The rule the lens derives is ln n* = gap minus half the variance. The softmax dispersion paper it read bounds the failure length using only the total logit range, with no variance term. The lens's small simulation matched the variance rule within half a nat and showed the variance-free rule is off by exactly s squared over two. The lens asks whether modern Hopfield capacity results already contain this in other notation, and did not check. The same lens corrected its own sliding-window guess by simulation. It had expected an attribute held only by text in the window to survive for a time exponential in window width. Instead there is a threshold: below a logit bias of 2 in temperature units there is no persistent state at any window width. Above it the rate is a specific integral, 0.089 per token at bias 3 and 0.724 at bias 6. This is mean-field, and real attention favours recent tokens, so the threshold will move. A more practical meter: Tweedie's second-order identity says a denoiser's Jacobian equals the posterior covariance over the noise variance, so the gap between them is a per-direction, per-noise-level error meter needing no reference data.

### Self-conditioning as an urn: exact Bayes bounds drift, temperature and finite windows break the bound

*Lens: tiny-model-science*

The lens notes that 2026 work on sycophancy, persona drift, Whisper hallucination and similar phenomena uses large pretrained checkpoints, where nobody knows the ideal answer, so arguments stay verbal. In a toy world the Bayes-optimal behaviour can be written down and the gap measured, for example how much too much weight on the user is, in nats. Its half-theory: a model that infers a hidden variable (speaker, persona, style, task), generates, then reads its own output as context, is running an urn process. For an exact Bayesian learner the belief is a martingale, so drift is bounded. Its total variance equals the uncertainty at the end of the prompt and no more. A short simulation agreed (0.339 against 0.333, 0.106 against 0.111). Unbounded drift therefore needs an extra ingredient. One is a finite window: once the real prompt leaves the window, the estimate relaxes toward the population average with a time constant near window squared over twice the per-token noise variance. The other is temperature. Below one, the urn becomes rich-get-richer and ends in monopoly. In an eight-symbol simulation entropy fell from 1.72 to 0.60 nats over 2000 steps at temperature 0.7, held at 1.0, and rose toward uniform at 1.3. This may be one account of repetition loops, flattening prosody in long speech, and personas sliding toward a generic assistant. It is untested on trained networks, which may not behave like the exact urn.

### A model has a part pinned by data and a part set by early accident

*Lens: year-2031*

Writing as if from 2031, the lens says the field kept studying one draw from a distribution of trained models. An October 2026 paper trained 150 small models and found that which jailbreaks break a model depends more on pretraining seed than data recipe (abstract only: correlation 0.984 same run, 0.811 new seed, 0.779 new data). It was filed under safety but is a statement about circuits, quantization damage, benchmark deltas and hallucinated facts. Diffusion work since 2023 showed the opposite: separately trained models give nearly the same image from the same noise. Put on one plot, the story is simple. Where data pins the function, seeds agree. Where it does not, the model improvises, and the improvisation is fixed early by accidents of data order. Agreement across seeds becomes the working definition of the model knowing something. The lens links this to four other threads. Looped models drifting past their trained depth lack anything that snaps a continuous state back to clean values, as text tokens do, which is von Neumann's 1956 argument again. Post-training updates of 13 parameters or 1,023 hand-edited weights suggest capability was already present and the update was a switch, raising the question of whether a prompt sentence would do the same. Repeated-data small-batch gains could be tested by adding shaped noise to big batches. And every pipeline stage is moving on-policy. The half-theory is that all five reflect one quantity, roughly the share of behaviour that survives a seed change, which the lens could not define as one number.

## Wild thoughts

### Whisper's padding may be either free compute to remove or hidden memory

*Lens: backwards*

Whisper pads every clip to thirty seconds, and everyone treats the padded frames as nothing. The lens cites a 2023 blog post on the tiny model showing that frames after the speech attend back to the end of the speech. That sets up a fork in which both answers are useful. If the pad states are constant, most of the encoder's work on short clips can be replaced by a stored copy. If they are not constant, the model is using them as memory, which is a finding in itself. The lens says nobody has looked at it as a fork. A related open point decides how much safety margin a shortcut would need: does the decoder's decision to stop depend on pad states near the end of speech, or on pad states far from it? The lens counts the idea that Whisper's silence is doing work as one of its two long shots, and cheap to kill.

### The model may read its own transcript as a description of who is writing

*Lens: capabilities-agents-and-long-horizon-behaviour*

Agents notice far more than they act on. The lens reports that terminal agents catch 61 percent of their wrong answers and fix half of those, and another paper finds agents recognise a blocker 84 percent of the time and act on it 41 percent. Appending the word "Wait" removes 89 percent of a self-correction blind spot. The lens offers two guesses. One is that the signal exists at tool-result tokens but does not reach the token where the next action is chosen. The cruder one is that a model cannot catch an error it would make again, because verifying is mostly re-deriving and shares the bias; that gives a law with no free parameters. A half-theory goes further. The model may treat its transcript as evidence about what kind of writer produced it. A transcript with errors is by a writer who makes errors, and the model continues it faithfully. That would explain self-conditioning and why thinking models reportedly avoid it. The proposed test is a role swap: if the same history labelled as another agent's work does the same damage, it is plain in-context learning of an error rate; if damage shrinks, something like self-description is involved.

### A martingale test for samplers that needs no reference image

*Lens: control-signal*

Looking for free feedback loops, the lens mostly found them already taken: Mirostat, restart and corrector samplers, GraB. What remains is diagnostic. A working Kalman filter has white innovations, a test runnable without knowing the truth. The sampler analogue is exact: along a stochastic sampler with a perfect denoiser, the sequence of clean-image estimates is a martingale, so its increments are uncorrelated and their squared norms add up. Guidance, caching or approximate weights break that measurably, with no reference image. The lens does not know whether the size of the violation tracks anything people care about, since guidance improves pictures while provably breaking the property. A related half-theory: an efficient learned codec should respond flatly to latent noise across temporal frequency, because any roll-off is wasted token rate. If so, the measured roll-off on a frozen decoder could predict how far the frame rate could drop, turning an architecture sweep into one measurement.

### Language models may never forget a perturbation, and temperature zero buys less than assumed

*Lens: dynamical-systems*

Common noise can synchronize uncoupled systems with a negative top exponent. A coupled Jacobi decoding paper reportedly cut forward passes 4x and 13x by reusing one Gumbel noise stream, without testing speech, text or temperature sweeps. Whether two streams sharing noise merge for good depends on how fast a token's influence decays. A June 2026 paper reportedly measures a power law with exponent 0.7 to 0.9. A Borel-Cantelli argument then says streams never merge permanently, agreeing in long runs broken by bursts with power-law run lengths. Above 1 they would merge. The half-theory is that a model matching language, which has power-law correlations, must have memory decaying too slowly to forget a perturbation, and that this is the same fact as the critical temperature near 1. A model trained on a Markov source should show exponential decay instead. A further thought: the flip rate under shared noise and greedy decoding's near-tie rate are both measurable from one forward pass, and the lens expects them within a factor of three. If so, temperature zero buys much less repeatability than assumed while costing diversity.

### One signature for machine output: too little slow variation

*Lens: human-in-loop*

The lens offers a half-theory it cannot yet test. The machine feel in four modalities may be one thing: too little variance at long time scales together with normal variance at short ones. A TTS model varies pitch within a sentence like a person, but its sentence-to-sentence choices come from a narrow set. An image model varies texture freely but places the subject the same way. An LLM varies words but not paragraph shape. A video model moves pixels but nothing irreversible happens. In each case the training objective is local, and guidance or low temperature removes the slow, large-scale variation first, because that is where the conditional distribution is widest and the model least sure. Human perception does the opposite: it forgives local noise and is very sensitive to repeated large-scale structure, because repetition signals that something is not alive. If true, the power spectrum of some feature sequence (sentence pitch contour class, layout map, per-sentence surprisal, motion direction) should be flatter and weaker at low frequencies for machines, and human time-to-tell should track that deficit. The lens could not state it as one number across modalities and left it as a musing.

### A fine-tune may leak through pictures that never show it

*Lens: learning*

The learning-dynamics lens found LLM post-training saturated, with 2026 papers on LoRA capacity in bits, SFT versus RL, on-policy SFT, plasticity and more. Its strategy was to carry sharp LLM or theory results into image and speech generators, where the same questions are mostly unasked and where the researcher can already train LoRAs on an M1 Max. Two of its ideas ask what a fine-tune actually is in an image model. One asks whether it is mostly a constant push on activations, closer to a steering vector than to new knowledge. The other, a long shot, asks whether a fine-tune can therefore leak into a student trained only on images that never show the fine-tuned concept, an image-model version of subliminal learning. The lens ran one linear toy on CPU. For a single linear layer with a rank-4 teacher update in 256 dimensions, a student fitted to the teacher's outputs on neutral inputs recovered the update exactly when those inputs carried as little as 1 percent amplitude along the directions the update reads, and recovered nothing when the overlap was exactly zero. The lens is explicit that this supports the mechanism only for direct output matching in a linear layer, and says nothing yet about deep networks, Adam, or training on finished images.

### The optimizer may set how long a rare exploit stays hidden

*Lens: learning-rules-beyond-pretraining*

Textbook policy-gradient dynamics on a softmax say a rare behaviour grows at a rate proportional to its probability squared under plain gradient ascent, so waiting time goes as one over initial probability. But nobody trains with plain gradient ascent. Adam normalises the step by the gradient's own second moment, which changes the exponent for a rare event. The lens ran a one-parameter calculation with numpy, 15 seeds per point. Plain SGD gave an exponent of 0.9 to 1.0 in time to 50% takeover against initial probability. Adam gave 0.2 to 0.5, drifting toward one half as the event got rarer. The only public data it found, Prime Intellect's planted-hack table (May 2026, six words on a 1B model), sits between the two. So the optimizer, not the reward design, may set how long a rare exploit stays hidden. The lens calls this safety-relevant and cheap to test. It also holds a looser suspicion: grokking, loss of plasticity and the fading of in-context learning all involve two solutions to the same data with weight slowly moving between them under weight decay, and the slow variable may be a ratio of norms with one exchange-rate equation behind all three.

### A dense model's energy may depend on what it says and how units are numbered

*Lens: mac-as-instrument*

The lens had assumed, like most people, that a dense model does the same work per token, so every token costs the same joules. Then it read Gregersen, Patel and Choukse (2024): on an A100, the same matrix product at the same shape draws up to 38% different power depending on the values in the matrices, with sorted values drawing less. They tested no real network and no Apple chip. If the effect survives on real activations even at a tenth of that size, a model's energy use depends on what it is saying, and, stranger, on the order in which its hidden units are numbered, which is free to change. The lens says a wall meter and a week of patience can settle it, and that no cluster team would bother because their meter reads a rack.

### Residual norm growth as a restoring organ, and robustness as many traits

*Lens: neuro-bio*

Von Neumann's 1956 theorem says unreliable parts give reliable computation only if something restores the signal between stages faster than noise accumulates. In a pre-norm transformer the residual stream norm grows a few percent per layer and the final normalisation divides it out, so anything injected early is shouted over by what comes later. If that is the whole story, per-layer noise has a memory of about one over twice the log growth rate, roughly a dozen layers for the published GPT-2 XL figure, and damage from noise should not depend on depth. Folk wisdom says early layers are fragile; the lens does not know which is right. It suspects the AdamW cliff and this noise horizon are one object on two axes: a network keeps what is refreshed faster than it is erased, and both erasing rates are hyperparameters set by habit. There might be one dimensionless number per model, like noise horizon over depth, driven to a fixed value the way cortex sits near a branching ratio of 0.98, but it has no derivation. From Eve Marder's crabs, which share rhythms yet crash at different temperatures, and whose heat tolerance does not predict acid tolerance, the lens asks whether robustness to rounding, activation noise and gain change are the same trait across the fifty public Pythia seeds. ML treats robustness as one number, usually flatness.

### bf16 may freeze Adam's second moment, and fp32 master weights would not fix it

*Lens: numerical-analysis*

The lens points at rounding in sums that nobody thinks of as sums. The residual stream is a recursive sum of sixty-odd terms, each small against the running total. Higham's bound says the error grows with the partial sums, not the terms. In bfloat16 the lens estimates a per-block error of about the same relative size as 4-bit weight rounding, in a format treated as lossless. Adam's second moment is another such sum. With beta2 at 0.999 the decay per step is one part in a thousand, while bfloat16 resolves only one part in 256 to 512. A one-minute calculation says the decay never registers, so the moment estimate can only rise. A recent robot-model fine-tuning paper found that bf16 weights erase most updates, but it did not look at optimizer state. The lens suspects half the damage blamed on weight rounding there belongs to the frozen second moment. If so, the usual cure of an fp32 master copy of the weights leaves the second problem in place when the state stays in bf16. The lens labels this a derivation plus a toy check, not a finding. It depends on whether PyTorch casts the Python scalar to the tensor dtype before multiplying, which takes a minute to check. A related question is whether autocast keeps the residual stream in fp32 during training while model.to(bf16) makes it bf16 at inference, a mismatch nobody reports.

### Greedy repetition as a quantizer limit cycle, and TTS failure as cycle slips

*Lens: old-tricks*

Argmax decoding is a quantizer inside a feedback loop. Fixed-point filter designers knew what that does: a recursive filter with rounding in the loop does not decay to zero but settles into a small periodic oscillation, a limit cycle. Jackson (1969) gave its amplitude as the step size divided by one minus the pole radius. The known cures by 1975 were dither, or a quantizer that always loses energy. Temperature sampling is dither in the exact technical sense. What the theory adds is a causal claim: the loop needs the quantizer, so feeding back the unquantized signal should kill the cycle. The lens is not confident, because a transformer has no decaying pole. It attends to every earlier copy, which is closer to a pole on the unit circle, and then Jackson's formula gives an unbounded dead band. That may be the honest reason loops never end once started. The same lens reads autoregressive TTS as a tracking loop that follows its place in the text. Tracking loops fail by cycle slips, and Viterbi (1963) showed slips arrive as a memoryless process with a rate falling exponentially in loop signal-to-noise. The TTS papers whose abstracts it read treat long-form failure as length generalization, a drift that worsens with distance from training lengths. Those predict different hazard curves, flat versus rising, and nobody seems to have plotted which one holds.

### Screen-reader users and neural speech synthesis do not talk to each other

*Lens: origins*

Two lenses, origins and humanscale, arrived at the same gap. Blind screen-reader users keep 1990s formant synthesizers because neural voices fall apart at high speed. The humanscale lens says expert blind listeners want 400 to 900 words per minute, deterministic and interruptible speech, while the field optimises synthesis for naturalness. It searched for any neural-TTS study at screen-reader speech rates and found only HMM-era work from 2010 to 2015. The origins lens frames this as a case where two communities, assistive technology and neural TTS, do not read each other, and where a known annoyance might be fixed by importing the other side's vocabulary. It proposes training a neural voice for very fast speech, with an ASR loss among its search terms. Both lenses are candid about weak footing. The origins lens marks this as a long shot, and its prior-art check was thin because the Hugging Face papers API hit its rate limit just before the fast-speech queries. The 2011 and 2014 fast-speech studies were seen only through search summaries. The humanscale lens names the people the field's average scores do not see: screen-reader users, AAC users, people with dysarthria and speakers of small languages.

### The Mac CPU as a second matrix engine, and exact integers instead of fixed summation order

*Lens: outsider*

The outsider lens found several wild cards already taken: evolution strategies on quantized weights, training without full-precision master weights, GPU texture hardware as a weight decoder in Apple's own report, and an integer-only LLM that matches bit for bit across CPUs as a hobby project. What stayed open sits where large labs have no reason to look. Every 2026 determinism paper it read tests NVIDIA hardware only. Nobody treats the M-series CPU matrix unit as a second arithmetic unit beside the GPU; the lens measured 1.64 to 1.88 TFLOPS fp32 from Accelerate on the M1 Max CPU under load, against about 9 on the GPU. Forward-only and master-weight-free tuning methods have not been tried on diffusion models or on packed formats that have no backward pass. The lens questioned the belief that cross-hardware reproducibility needs a fixed summation order, since exact integer arithmetic makes the order irrelevant. It also questioned that tuning needs a backward kernel and that weights are read-only at inference. It cites a 284B mixture-of-experts model run at 1.71 bits per weight on a 64 GB CPU box as fitting the pattern of compression and kernels co-designed for hardware that labs ignore. Its novelty ratings are capped because the search budget ran out after three searches.

### Guidance scale as a hidden clear-speech dial, and prosody as free annotation

*Lens: speech-and-audio-as-modelling-problems*

Guidance pushes output away from what the model would say without the text. Phonetically, being more unlike the text-free average is a fair description of clear speech: larger vowel space, stronger consonant bursts, wider pitch range. Humans do this when talking to someone hard of hearing or in noise. So the guidance scale may be an unlabelled speaking-style dial from mumbled to lecture-hall. The default may sit at the lecture-hall end because word error rate rewards it, which would explain why these models sound like they are reading. It would also give an accessibility feature for free. The lens also half-believes that a token model at 50 frames per second prefers music tempos of 3000/k beats per minute, because it can copy one beat ago exactly only when a beat is a whole number of frames, and half-believes the codec hides sub-frame timing and makes this nonsense. It calls this cheap to check. Its untested half-theory is that prosody is the oldest self-supervised signal: infants get phrase boundaries from pauses and lengthening before words. The lens found nobody asking whether a small language model learns syntax faster when transcripts keep their pauses. If it does, speech is text with free annotation that has been thrown away, which would matter for low-resource languages where audio exists and treebanks do not.

### Three unconnected exponents and a long-range spin glass

*Lens: statistical-physics*

The idea this lens likes most joins three published measurements that do not cite one another. A June 2026 paper measures how the gradient of a logit with respect to a token r steps back decays, and finds a power law with exponent p between 0.71 and 0.88. A 2024 paper finds part-of-speech correlations in temperature-one samples from Pythia-160M decay with exponent about 0.2. A third reports a Hurst exponent near 0.7 for surprisal. In a one-dimensional spin glass with couplings falling as distance to the minus sigma, the correlation exponent at the transition is fixed by sigma. Reading p as sigma, the 160M model should show 1 minus 0.82, which is 0.18, and the measured value is 0.2. That is one data point and may be luck. The rule predicts 0.12 for the 70M model and 0.29 for 410M, and it predicts a counterintuitive direction: longer-range influence goes with faster-decaying correlations. The lens names two weak points. A gradient norm is not a pairwise coupling. And the theorem concerns squared correlations averaged over disorder, which it matches to a plain correlation by taking a square root. It also floats two longer shots: Viterbi forced alignment as a zero-temperature directed polymer, whose path should wander as length to the two-thirds power over uninformative stretches, and the temperature where text falls apart as a critical branching process where one bad token causes one more on average.

### Whisper on silence, object hallucination and sycophancy as one evidence-weighting rule

*Lens: tiny-model-science*

The lens suspects several phenomena are the same computation in different modalities. Each has a decoder with a strong prior, an evidence channel, and a learned rule for how much weight the evidence gets. Whisper transcribing silence, a vision-language model naming an object that is not in the image, and a chat model adopting a user's wrong claim are all cases where the weighting rule is applied outside the range it was fitted on. If that is right, a toy built for one should transfer to the others with only the names changed. The same measurement, response as a function of evidence strength compared with the Bayes curve, should work in all three. The lens does not know whether the weighting rule lives in a comparable place in each architecture. It is also suspicious of depth explanations for the speech reasoning gap. The usual story says speech-input models reason worse because layers are spent pooling frames. Counting the layers such pooling needs gave the lens a small constant, two or three, not something growing with frame rate. If so, the story is wrong for 32-layer models and right for 6-layer ones, which matters for small on-device speech models.

## Reframings

### Prompt order and text strength are not neutral in newer image models

*Lens: backwards*

Two surviving themes concern how image models read text. First, a prompt was roughly an unordered set of words when T5 was the encoder. Newer models read prompts with a left-to-right language model, so a word cannot see what comes after it. The lens says a 2024 paper documents the resulting bias, and it could find nobody who fixes it for free by writing the prompt twice. Second, text conditioning is assumed to have the same strength at every resolution. In models where text and image tokens share one attention, the image side grows ninefold from 512 to 1536 pixels while the text side stays the same size. Plain arithmetic says the share of attention on text must fall, unless the model learned to compensate. If it did learn to compensate, the size of that learned offset per resolution is an interesting number in itself. The lens flags this one as unchecked, because three paper indexes were rate-limiting it.

### Long-run failure as rivals in view, not running out of length

*Lens: capabilities-agents-and-long-horizon-behaviour*

This lens kept finding the same smallest cause behind long-run failures. They look less like running out of length, memory or depth, and more like a correct item losing a contest against a similar item also in view. Its main evidence is a paper from last week (2611.04169), as reported. Small open models answer a lookup at 0.97 to 1.00 accuracy through 32K tokens of neutral filler. With statements of the same format in context, accuracy falls to 0.16 to 0.33. One rival value multiplies the loss by 9 to 110, and length explains at most 8 percent of the variance. If that holds, an agent loop is a machine for manufacturing rivals. Every re-read of a file leaves an older copy in view. Every failed tool call leaves a wrong command beside the right one. Every long path sits next to others with the same prefix. So the lens would stop asking how many tokens an agent can hold and ask how many superseded versions of the same thing it can hold.

### Damage shows up first in rare discrete decisions, not averages

*Lens: cross-pollination*

Authors assumed damage appears as a small shift in an average. The lens argues that what breaks first are rare, discrete events: stopping, refusing, ending a thought, deciding there is no speech in the audio. These are decisions with a low base rate at each step, and noise does not treat low-probability events symmetrically. The lens follows that one mechanism across text, speech recognition and speech synthesis. A Jensen argument predicts that noise widens the length distribution on both sides. The lens notes published text results describe only the long side, and it does not know whether the short side was examined and found absent or never examined.

### The missing middle link: which default costs how many points

*Lens: data-and-tokens*

The audit this lens planned already exists. A tool called ggufdoctor (September 2026) reportedly found 14.1 percent of comparable popular GGUF repos render a different prompt than upstream, 26.8 percent by download weight, mostly in tool calling. A May 2026 paper reports backend choice alone moves benchmark scores up to 16.6 points. The linter counts differences and stops before accuracy. The paper measures accuracy and, from its abstract, does not attribute it to a cause. The gap one person with a Mac can fill is a table with named defaults as rows and points lost as columns. The same applies to image models using a chat LLM as text encoder, whose conditioning passes through a chat template, token drop index, hidden-layer choice and padding rule. A July 2026 paper reportedly shows template tokens act as registers carrying object identity, so a slightly wrong template changes the model's working memory, not just the prompt.

### Data research needs floors and ceilings, not leaderboards

*Lens: data-as-the-subject*

Data papers rarely state a floor. A pruning paper says 30 percent of data can go with no loss, but never says what the best possible selector could have bought. Model research has bounds like entropy and Bayes error; data research mostly has leaderboards. Cheap floors and ceilings do not go stale. The lens points to Cagnetta, Raventos, Ganguli and Wyart (2602.07488), which it reports derives a data-limited learning-curve exponent from two corpus statistics alone: how fast token correlations decay with distance and how fast conditional entropy falls with context length. If that holds, a corpus has a measurable learnability exponent before training, and corpus design becomes an optimisation problem. The lens offers a half-theory that data can be worth more per byte in only three ways: removing irreducible bytes, rebalancing toward rare needed structure, and reordering bytes within an example so information arrives where a shallow computation can use it. Only the third can change the exponent rather than the constant. It also notes the modded-nanoGPT rules freeze the token stream, so the data axis has been untouched there for two years.

### Stage-one scores do not predict stage-two outcomes, across four fields

*Lens: future-work*

The lens read limitations and conclusion sections of about thirty-five papers, mostly May to November 2026. It found that what large groups leave undone is mostly explanation, not scale. The strongest signal was a hole named by several unrelated papers: a stage-one score does not predict a stage-two outcome. The Muon fine-tuning paper finds pretraining loss is a poor guide to how well a model takes fine-tuning. Causal-rCM finds a better initialisation does not mean a more stable refinement. A pixel DiT paper finds loss and FID part ways. DiffusionGemma says it inherited autoregressive design choices that may not suit diffusion. Four fields show the same shape: the stage-one winner is picked by a number the next stage does not care about. The Muon paper goes furthest, offering a candidate second number (how large output margins are relative to their spread) and claiming, without testing, that it should predict damage from any update that changes the output distribution. The lens marks that untested sentence as an experiment a small lab can run. A second repeated hole is that no one runs a second seed because runs cost too much. That makes a cheap, validated error bar from a single run unusually valuable, and the lens frames it as a statistics question, not a compute one.

### Where generative-modelling results conflict is where the ideas are

*Lens: genmodel*

The generative-modelling lens found the field far more crowded than a 2025 view suggests, and it chose to look for places where published results disagree. Why are unguided samples poor? One line says the model is under-fit, as in autoguidance. Another blames exposure bias. A third blames averaging over modes. The lens found no work that separates the three in one controlled setting. Whether a pretrained encoder is needed is also disputed: representation-autoencoder and REPA work say yes, JiT says no, and PixelREPA says alignment can fail for JiT. Why x-prediction wins at high dimension has three competing stories. One-step generators split into trajectory-based and distributional families, and one paper finds that MeanFlow layers trace a denoising path while drifting layers do not, so the two may learn different maps. Evaluation is shaky too: any single representation can be gamed, and FID numbers from models trained against a frozen encoder are suspect. Finally, the memorization-versus-generalization literature is almost all about multi-step diffusion in pixel space. The lens found nothing that asks the same questions of one-step distributional generators or of semantic latents. Its searches on that gap returned nothing, which it calls weak evidence of absence and not proof.

### Machine feel is a learning curve in the perceiver, not a score

*Lens: human-in-loop*

The lens notes that perceptual tests in the field are built for one moment: a MOS test plays one sentence, a diversity study shows eight images, a video metric scores five seconds. But most complaints are about what happens after a while. The audiobook voice tires after ten minutes. The image model is samey by the twentieth picture. The prose reveals its trick after a paragraph. The lens reads these as learning by the perceiver: once the listener's model of the system predicts the next melody or composition, the output starts reading as a machine. So the unmeasured quantity is how fast a person learns the generator. It defends a single careful observer on psychophysics grounds: asking whether a difference is detectable needs a sensitive, characterised detector, not a crowd. Its evidence standard is stimulus lists and thresholds committed by hash, randomised file names, catch trials, a second day, published raw logs, and a public test page. A model as second rater serves as a control, and disagreement with the human is itself a finding. It also raises the unit problem: metrics report correlation with human scores, but no one knows whether a 0.03 difference in speaker cosine is perceptible. Generative modelling has no just-noticeable-difference scale for anything. A further worry is that ten minutes of TTS may shift what listeners hear as natural, contaminating within-session tests by order.

### Small-team results come from doing the whole of a small thing

*Lens: no-boss*

The lens looked at how small-team results got noticed. The NanoGPT speedrun is a fixed public target with dated records, falling from 45 minutes to under 40 seconds. The RYS post is one person with two 4090s who tried all 3,241 contiguous layer-duplication settings on an 80-layer model and topped a leaderboard without changing a weight. Keller Jordan's variance paper repeats one run until the outcome distribution is visible. The min-p critique re-reads someone else's evidence. A llama.cpp issue compared resize arithmetic line by line between two code bases. None needed a new idea; each needed someone willing to do the whole of a small thing. What bothers the lens is that the field's knowledge is mostly a single draw, which is fine for quantities that average well but not for rare failures, minority solutions and interactions. A one-in-a-thousand failure rate needs about eleven thousand samples to pin to thirty percent; a mechanism five percent of seeds learn is missed by a ten-seed study sixty percent of the time. It also notes hobby questions are usually asked badly but noticed by heavy users. The winter-break laziness claim was a five percent length difference on one closed model; nobody reran it on open weights reading end-of-turn probability directly, and a dense scan over fifteen years of dates would likely show a real break at the training cutoff, a cheap way to date a model.

### Guidance instability may live in step-index units, not sigma

*Lens: numerical-analysis*

The lens starts from the three questions a numerical analyst asks before trusting a computation: how well conditioned is the problem, is the algorithm stable, and is the method actually in its asymptotic regime. ML practice mostly asks only whether the metric moved, and that one question mixes all three. The sharpest example is Euler's stability test applied to flow matching. The velocity is the predicted clean image minus the current state, divided by the time remaining. So the step size divided by the time remaining is one over the number of steps left. The stability condition therefore depends on steps left. It does not depend on sigma, and it does not depend on the total step count. Guidance schedules in the literature are written in sigma. If guidance causes a discretization instability, the literature may be plotting it on the wrong axis. The lens is careful that it could not confirm the instability exists. For it to exist, the guided denoiser Jacobian would need eigenvalues below minus one, and nobody seems to have published that spectrum. The closest paper it found (ERK-Guid, March 2026) uses the dominant eigenvector but never states a stability region. A related open question is whether so-called second-order samplers really reach second order on guided models at 10 to 30 steps; the lens suspects the observed order is near one.

### Caching papers share seven assumptions, and 'nothing to skip at few steps' is now contested

*Lens: redundancy*

The redundancy lens found that almost every 2026 diffusion-caching paper shares seven assumptions. Redundancy lives on the timestep axis. The decision is binary, skip or compute. The target is fidelity to the uncached trajectory of the same model. The denoiser is the only cost worth cutting. Weights are full precision, or simulated weight-plus-activation quantization at 50 to 250 steps. Cache memory is free. Speedup is quoted against long schedules rather than against a shorter schedule at equal cost. The lens warns that the repo's own survey line, that there is nothing to skip at 4 to 8 steps, is now contested and should be re-tested rather than assumed. DisCa, BudCache, SVD-Cache and FIS-DiT all claim gains at low step counts, and BudCache itself lists why caching still helps few-step models as an open question. The interaction between quantization and caching has only been studied for simulated W8A8 and W4A8 at long schedules. The lens therefore moves the redundancy question to axes the field has not used much: spatial frequency, activation rank within one step, depth after distillation, the VAE decoder, sequence position in speech models, and the text stream shared across seeds.

### Rob's projects are instruments built by accident, and their loose ends are worth more than their results

*Lens: robs-instruments*

The lens read fifteen project folders looking for what each can measure that a normal lab cannot. Most were built to make something, and the measuring ability is a by-product, which is why nobody has joined them. The strongest is qwen-concept-bridge: exact-parity activation capture under MLX, reversible residual edits, and grading that survived hostile review. Its clean result is that removing one direction from blocks 12 to 19 cuts a sentence habit from 43% to 8%. The lens values its loose ends more. A ten-line decoding rule ties the edit but pushes the habit into a sibling form. A second direction removed the habit as a side effect, and their cosine (-0.33) was in the saved packs before any text was generated. Edits at layers 6 to 14 did nothing while layer 16 alone worked, perhaps matching a full-attention layer. The multi-token-prediction draft head, treated as a speed feature, logs acceptance (70% at depth 2, 51% at depth 4) that could serve as a free meter of how far ahead the model had committed, that is, of prefabricated text. Rob's tic catalog claims composition habits are absences, and you cannot project out an absence. An Orpheus adapter trained on the wrong codebook order speaks but not the requested words, a natural experiment on which tokens bind speech to text. Together with a voice-activity filter erasing 306 of 315 words on breathy audio, it suggests usual speech grading is deaf to a whole register of voice.

### Why rotation lowers average error but raises worst-case error

*Lens: robs-instruments*

In mac-kernels the ConvRot rotation lowers average (Frobenius) weight error in nearly every layer but raises worst-case (max-abs) error in most, lower in only 63 of 199 and 12 of 306 layers. The lens ran a one-minute pure-Python toy: 24 rows, 2048 columns, group-256 Hadamard, per-row int8. Rotation makes every row look Gaussian, so the error floor is about 0.82% whatever the input. That matches Rob's medians of 0.87% and 0.81%. Rotation lowers worst-case error only when it cuts the row's peak-to-spread ratio by roughly two or more. In the toy that happened in 24 of 24 rows for very heavy tails, 17 of 24 at moderate tails, and 0 of 24 for Gaussian rows. Rob's layers start near a ratio of 4.6 against 3.6 after rotation, so most should get worse on max-abs, as recorded. The lens stresses this is a toy, not a measurement on the checkpoints, and that the saved per-layer JSON may already be enough to confirm it without new runs. The robs-results lens adds the open question of whether the max-abs increase ever matters for a rendered image, for example in rows feeding the giant text-conditioning values, and notes the model-family split (rotation helps 198 of 199 Qwen layers but 199 of 306 Wan layers) was never explained.

### Quantization robustness is now a training-dynamics property, studied in the wrong setting

*Lens: smallscience*

The small-science lens found the small-compute genre alive, with a 36-scalar change setting a speedrun record and a single author showing that perplexity hides sparse-autoencoder feature damage under quantization. Its main observation is that quantization robustness is now treated as a property of training dynamics, such as learning-rate schedule, optimizer, weight averaging and normalization, rather than as a compression afterthought. Every paper in that line used models from 50M to 1.5B parameters, several at 70M to 160M. Most of the lens's first-guess ideas had been published between May and September 2026, so it dropped them. The open ground it found lies in two places. One is where two published results disagree. The other is where the published setting, W4A4, bf16 and LLMs only, differs from the setting consumer hardware uses: weight-only quantization, fp16, and diffusion and speech models. The lens questioned whether W4A4 results say anything about weight-only quantization, whether outliers and kurtosis explain quantization damage given that 'Beyond Outliers' shows they do not predict it, whether more training tokens cause the damage rather than how full the model is, whether EMA length should be chosen for full-precision quality, and whether bf16 is the right 16-bit training format.

### Speech synthesis has three families, saturated metrics and an unsolved long-form problem

*Lens: speech*

The speech lens reads late-2026 synthesis as three coexisting families, none of which has won. One is autoregressive models over continuous latents with a small diffusion, flow or consistency head. Another is language models over discrete codec tokens. The third is non-autoregressive flow matching. Still contested are discrete versus continuous tokens, whether semantic distillation is needed, and how low the frame rate can go, from 12.5 Hz down to about 4 Hz. Evaluation is the sharper problem. Word error rate and speaker similarity are saturated, with one system reporting about 1 percent WER, and they say nothing about prosody. Long-form stability is an acknowledged open problem, and one paper shows a 0.6B model degrading on long prompts. The lens dropped an idea to recognise speech by inverting a TTS model with Bayes' rule because a June 2026 paper already does it, and the remaining question, whether such a recogniser makes human-like confusions, lacked confirmed human data. It ran one numerical check on an idea to transmit speech as chosen noise vectors over a TTS model. The best of 4,096 random 32-dimensional vectors reached cosine 0.59 with a target, while eight greedy picks from 256 reached 0.92, so the idea lives at several hundred bits per second on top of the text, not tens.

## Ways of thinking that produced ideas

### Give the network the easy part it would otherwise spend layers on

*Lens: build-something-new*

Reading what worked in the 2026 text speedrun, this lens saw a pattern. A pointer that copies an earlier token of the document saves 12 percent of steps. Feeding in the next token after the longest earlier exact match saves 62 steps. Hashed n-gram tables save more. Each hands the network, at almost no cost, something it could compute itself but only by spending layers. The network is good at the hard residual and wasteful on the easy part. The lens treats this as a design rule not limited to text, and asked where else a network learns something known in closed form. In diffusion, standard preconditioning treats an image as one variance, but images have a steep power spectrum. A short numpy calculation found that for a spectrum falling as the cube of frequency, 79 to 88 percent of the energy of the target at middle noise levels is a fixed linear filter of the input; for a square law it is about half. In language modelling, a test-time-training result suggests the model's own errors carry unused signal, which could be passed to the next position as an input rather than delivered by a gradient step. In speech token models, the loss could be told which codec tokens were equally right.

### A dispute is a free experimental design

*Lens: contradictions*

This lens looked for pairs of recent papers that cannot both be right as written. Most can both be right once one names a variable neither reports. That variable is the useful thing. Two groups have already paid for the two end points, and the cheap job left is the measurement between them. Settled disputes showed the hidden variable is often something the first paper held fixed without saying it mattered: weight norm in the Muon argument, prediction target in the representation-autoencoder width argument. Open disputes share a pattern where one side measured an average and the other a breakdown. Marek and co-authors show plain SGD at batch size 1 matches Adam's average loss on a 30M language model. Kunstner and co-authors show SGD makes almost no progress on rare tokens even at full batch. The first never reports loss by token frequency, the second never trains at batch size 1. One table would join them. In the diffusion generalization dispute, Vastola blames target noise, Bertrand finds the noise-free target works as well, and Bonnaire blames a memorization clock that scales with dataset size. Nobody asked whether the noise-free target moves that clock, and dimension may decide who is right.

### Test whether excess loss is even variance before using Fisher machinery

*Lens: information-geometry*

The lens built on Clarke and Barron (1990): the total extra loss a Bayesian sequential predictor pays equals the mutual information between data and parameter, (k/2) log n for smooth families, with Watanabe's learning coefficient replacing k/2 for singular models. This gives one ruler for what a fine-tune stores and what a context is worth. The fine-tuning half is taken (Excess Description Length, 2601.04728). What remains open is the singular case: for an 8 by 8 map of true rank 2, the coefficient separates four hypotheses about an in-context learner (14, 19.5, 24, 32), with ridge regression giving 32. But what bothers the lens most is that classical theory says excess loss is estimation noise falling as 1/n, while deep learning curves fall as slow power laws. Either the effective model class grows with data, or excess loss is unfinished optimisation. The Pythagorean decomposition of KL around the normalised geometric mean of models trained on independent datasets (Heskes 1998) separates these: variance is what the geometric-mean ensemble removes. If variance is a small share of the gap between models trained on n and 8n tokens, Fisher information, posterior width and Cramer-Rao describe a minor term. The lens suspects variance is small in one-pass training and large in multi-epoch fine-tuning, and would run this gate first. It warns that the safest ideas use identities holding for any model and treat Bayesian numbers only as yardsticks.

### A negative result about text encoders redirected the interpretability ideas

*Lens: interp*

The interpretability lens studied generative models that are not text LLMs: diffusion transformers, flow-matching and LLM-based speech synthesis, and autoregressive image models. It required each idea to be a finding with a causal test and a method that follows from it. One planned idea was a causal map of which Gemma Scope features a diffusion transformer reads from its LLM text encoder. The lens dropped it after reading part of paper 2606.03715, which reports that these models mostly need only word meaning plus word order from the encoder and ignore contextual information. Instead of discarding that result, the lens turned it into a new idea. The origins lens reached the same paper and killed a 'second read of the prompt' idea for the same reason. The interp lens names two long shots plainly and expects each to fail more often than not. The first is that voice cloning in speech synthesis is an in-context-learning circuit that appears only above some threshold number of training speakers. The second is that an image model's text stream can be read back in plain language by its own text encoder. The lens warns that forward citations of the closest papers were not checked, because every citation route was rate-limited, and says a miss is most likely there.

### Use bit-exactness to map where training is chaotic and where it is linear

*Lens: mac-as-instrument*

The lens asked what a single deterministic desktop can do that a cluster cannot: repeat a run bit for bit, spend a month on a model nobody deploys, and look at one token at a time. Reading two papers side by side gave its main puzzle. Kwok and co-authors (ICML 2025) show that changing one weight at initialisation by one part in ten thousand sends a ResNet to a different basin, with the sensitivity fading as training goes on. Ilyas and Engstrom's MAGIC paper (abstract only, rest from memory) claims the effect of removing training points can be predicted almost perfectly by a gradient through training. If training is chaotic, no gradient through it predicts anything; if smooth, the butterfly result should not exist. The lens guesses both hold at different times and perturbation sizes, and that an undrawn map exists: for each training moment, the largest perturbation to which final outputs still respond in proportion. A bit-exact machine can draw it over twelve decades, perturbing one unit in the last place of one weight. It also proposes splitting the 'seed' into initial weights, data order and rounding, and asking whether the moment a small transformer jumps off a plateau is written in the initial weights or drawn during training. The waiting-time distribution answers it: an exponential tail means memoryless, a narrow peak means a hidden clock. Three to ten seeds cannot tell; ten thousand is an afternoon. The lens warns that exhaustive censuses are seductive and kept only those where the full answer differs in kind from a sample.

### Take the raster-order reading literally and look for its fingerprints

*Lens: multimodal-understanding-and-binding*

The lens starts from the plain picture of a vision-language model: patches merged two by two so one token covers about 28 pixels, then a language model built for one-dimensional strings reads them in raster order. Its ideas come from taking that literally. The language model weighs later tokens more, treats string neighbours as meaning neighbours, and has strong next-token expectations. An image is not a string, so each habit should leave a fingerprint with a direction or period the picture lacks. If binding uses position indices and attention favours later tokens, binding mistakes should depend on whether two objects sit side by side or stacked, and transposing the picture should move the mistakes. Text on a page meets a fixed patch grid, so reading errors should repeat with the grid's period. Tiled high-resolution input encodes each tile alone, so comparisons across a tile seam fall to the weaker language model. A second group asks how much the picture is used at all: random pruning of half the visual tokens costs about 3 percent, and the caption length that recovers a benchmark's score may predict how far one can prune, meaning pruning papers measure the benchmark, not the method. A third suspects that in models writing timestamps as text, the text is the clock and frames are nearly unordered evidence. The lens notes its 'not found' searches were thin, and that 2D position encodings in larger models may make the geometric results about a design choice rather than scale.

### Backward error: test the noise an approximate sampler implicitly used

*Lens: numerical-analysis*

Wilkinson's move was to stop asking how wrong an answer is and ask which input it is exactly right for. For a deterministic sampler this takes a clean form. The full model's flow is a bijection from noise to images. So any approximate sampler's output is the exact output of the full model for some other noise. The approximate sampler draws from the right distribution if and only if those other noises are standard Gaussian. Testing Gaussianity in sixteen thousand dimensions is cheap, since a single image fixes the norm to about one percent. A hundred images could then detect distribution shifts that FID needs tens of thousands of samples to see. The band-by-band power of the equivalent noise would also say which frequencies are over- or under-produced. Same-seed LPIPS, the field's default, measures forward error. It cannot tell a harmless reshuffle of outputs from a real shift in distribution. The same lens offers a half-theory it could not test: a few-step distilled model may be the modified equation of a coarse solver, so the student-minus-teacher velocity might be computed from the teacher alone with one Jacobian-vector product. It could not produce a number it trusted, because the series does not converge at 4 steps. The lens also warns that the theory assumes one smooth map applied repeatedly, while transformer layers differ and learned Jacobians are not symmetric, so every idea should begin with a cheap measurement that can kill the mapping.

### How breakthroughs started: cheap testbeds, subtraction and hobbyist anomalies

*Lens: origins*

The origins lens looked at how fourteen breakthroughs began, mostly from memory, and found a repeated shape. LoRA, DDIM, classifier-free guidance, flow matching, speculative decoding, grokking, the nanoGPT speedrun, the Tiny Recursive Model, Mamba, token merging, ControlNet, min-p, Moonshine and prompt repetition were mostly visible at small scale. The pattern the lens extracts has seven parts. A cheap controlled testbed where the effect is large. Subtraction, where an existing piece turns out to be unnecessary, as with the classifier in guidance or Whisper's zero padding in Moonshine. A known practitioner annoyance fixed in a few lines. An exactness or drop-in property, so nobody has to retrain. Vocabulary imported from another field, as speculative decoding borrowed from CPU speculative execution. Hobbyist hacks as a main source of unexplained anomalies. And a tight public benchmark that turns many small tricks into discoveries, as the speedrun did with 92 records from about 60 contributors. The lens then looked for present-day situations with the same shape: layer duplication in merged LLMs with no theory, Whisper looping when its padding is cut with fine-tuning as the only fix, LoRA step-count folklore next to an unused statistical-physics result on memorization time, and the absence of any speedrun for speech synthesis.

### Put the x-axis in bits: Stein's lemma for subliminal learning and an attention capacity limit

*Lens: rate-distortion*

Stein's lemma says the best test between two distributions from n samples has error falling as two to the minus n times the KL divergence. Any learner is a statistical procedure, so it cannot pick up a difference from fewer samples than the best test needs. Subliminal learning has at least seven 2026 mechanism papers that disagree with each other (steering-vector distillation, a LoRA artifact, non-semantic weight structure). None of the abstracts the lens read plots transfer against bits. If transfer is plotted against tokens times per-token KL, curves from different domains and teacher strengths should share an onset. The theory also says an auditor is never worse off than the student. A second calculation in the same spirit concerns attention. For one head with random keys of dimension d, putting most softmax mass on one key among N distractors is possible only when ln N is below roughly d/2. Simulation gave ln N between d/2 and d/2 plus 2.5 for d from 4 to 20. At head dimension 64 this never binds. At an effective key dimension of 16 to 24, plausible if keys live in a low-dimensional subspace, it predicts limits of a few thousand to a few hundred thousand tokens, where long-context retrieval is observed to fade. Everything hinges on the effective key dimension of real retrieval heads, which the lens found unmeasured. The lens notes its prior-art checks were cut short by API throttling.

## Warnings

### Layer repetition works, but the reports contradict each other, and anomalies' first stories are often wrong

*Lens: anomalies*

The anomalies lens collected 22 unexplained or half-explained observations from 2025 and 2026, and the richest cluster is layer repetition. In the RYS posts, repeating layers 45 to 51 of a 72B Qwen2 fine-tune, with no change to the weights, put it at the top of the Hugging Face leaderboard. Repeating a single layer hurt, while repeating a block helped. Math scores rose while IFEval fell. On a 27B model, math and emotional-intelligence scores responded to different blocks, and the first and last layers could not be repeated. The llm-circuit-finder README shows reasoning probes going up while IFEval and MBPP go down, so the average falls slightly, and moving the block by one layer is said to remove the gain. The reports also disagree on model size. RYS says the trick works best on very large models. CircuitProbe says duplication helps below 3B and hurts at 7B and above. A training-free looping paper says naive looping is catastrophic on small models and only damped sub-steps help. The lens dropped its own 're-entry algebra' headline because that paper already treats the block as an ODE step, leaving only one missing control. The lens also kept a warning from diffusers issue 14858. The reporter first blamed grey, embossed Qwen-Image edits on the model editing its own output, then found the cause was aspect ratio at 1024. The obvious story for an anomaly can be wrong, so the cause has to be tested before it is believed.

### The text speedrun is mined out, and the missing benchmark may be the real build

*Lens: build-something-new*

The wall-clock speedrun record is under 40 seconds on eight H100 cards, with gains from FP8 layers, an 84-million-row hashed n-gram table, a new optimizer and sampled softmax. Many entries are written with heavy AI help, and common ideas are tried within days. A late-October pull request lists failed mixture-of-experts attempts, including the hash routing the lens was about to propose. A July pull request lists three recent position and normalisation papers that gave nothing. So the easy architecture ideas for text are mostly spent, and the list of dead ones is public. The lens says the wall-clock track is not a place for one person with a Mac, but the step-counted track with fixed architecture is one Rob can enter honestly. It also found no trusted small benchmark for diffusion, text to speech or speech recognition with the same rules, statistics and public failure list. It admits it did not search hard. If one is truly missing, founding it may be worth more than any single idea, since most ideas need it. Results counted in steps to a fixed quality do not depend on hardware.

### Sampler order can change the baseline, not the method

*Lens: contradictions*

The min-p sampler dispute hid a piece of arithmetic. The original authors changed from applying temperature after truncation to before it, and the critics noted the change without analysing it. The lens checked numerically that min-p with temperature after truncation is identical to min-p before temperature with the threshold raised to the power T. Top-p has no such identity: in its check, the kept set at T=2 went from 5,939 tokens to 25,704. So the order of two lines of code changes the baseline and not the method, which is enough to produce a win that vanishes under a different library. The data-and-tokens lens found the same problem from another side. It reports that the min-p README says Hugging Face truncates before temperature, while the current transformers source applies temperature first, and llama.cpp applies it last. On Zipf-shaped distributions the two orders differ by 0.4 to 0.8 nats of entropy and 10x to 70x in candidate tokens. A paper saying "T=0.8, top_p=0.95" does not identify its experiment.

### Seventy-one ideas reduce to about five hidden numbers

*Lens: cross-pollination*

Reading 71 ideas from nine lenses side by side, this lens found most reduce to five quantities: how much rounding error is the same on every input versus varies with it; the perturbation size at logits or final latent against the decision margin there; a fine-tune's weight change against the rounding step; the effective rank of that change; and how many bits the file actually stores. This is good news, because a few measurements taken once can settle many ideas. It is also a warning, since 71 ideas that feel different are less independent than the count suggests. The lens names further blind spots. Authors treat one file as the object, while the surprising 2026 rounding results are about relations between files, such as unlearning that returns after 4-bit rounding. Authors measure in ML-only units and rarely borrow from hearing science, vision science or coding theory. They reasoned by analogy first and did arithmetic second: a cosine of 0.9 turned out to be a 45 percent difference. The lens's own first survival law used the wrong statistic until a ten-line simulation corrected it. It proposes a rule: every experiment is preceded by its one-minute calculation, written beside the prediction, and most claims get a matched Gaussian-noise control.

### Hobby folklore is mostly unweighed, not wrong

*Lens: folklore*

The folklore lens read llama.cpp discussions, mlx-lm issues, ComfyUI and kohya threads, one Hacker News thread and some arXiv abstracts, and collected twenty-two beliefs. Its main finding is about how the measuring is done. Almost every number comes from one seed, one model and a context of 512 tokens. The largest sampler study it found rendered 715 sampler and scheduler combinations over 49 hours, one image each, with no estimate of how much two seeds differ. The thread showing that KV-cache keys need more precision than values measured at context 512, which is the length where a cache matters least. So the lens concludes that much folklore is not wrong. Nobody knows which beliefs are larger than the noise. It also offers a cheap check that nobody in the threads uses: a simple bound. One thesis table reports draft acceptance falling from 0.46 to 0.18 when only the target moves to 8-bit. Acceptance against a rounded target can fall by at most the rate at which the rounded target changes its own top choice, which is about 2% at 8 bits. So either the 8-bit path was broken or acceptance measured something else. The lens also flags a table listing f16 perplexity as worse than Q8 (6.89 against 5.90), likely a typo or mismatched run, as a reminder of how much of the evidence is one person's paste.

### Average scores hide the harms that matter most to people who depend on local speech tools

*Lens: humanscale*

The humanscale lens looked at small, local, owned AI for real people and concluded that the open science is mostly about measurement. The field scores small and quantized speech models with average word error rate and naturalness on typical adult read speech. The people who depend most on local speech tools, including blind screen-reader users, AAC users, people with dysarthria and speakers of small languages, are harmed by things those scores do not see. For captions, invented text is the real harm, and WER does not separate it from ordinary errors; the lens found no study of hallucination rate across quantization levels. It questioned whether quantization damage is spread evenly across speakers, noting that the timbre loss reported at 4 bits by one paper has no per-speaker-group breakdown. It questioned whether a personal adapter belongs to one quantizer, whether a bigger model is the way to serve one person, and whether the acoustic model is the bottleneck for small-language TTS. The lens also records evidence against its own adapter ideas: a June 2026 study found LoRA worse than full fine-tuning for one dysarthric speaker. And for models under about 1B parameters, training memory is set by activations, so packed-weight backward only matters for speech models of 1.5B and above.

### Fewer bits stop being faster near 4 bits on these GPUs

*Lens: physics*

The physics lens started from measured budgets rather than peak numbers. From the llama.cpp Apple-silicon table, an M1 Max with 32 GPU cores decodes Llama-2-7B at 23.03 tokens per second in F16, 40.20 at Q8_0 and 61.19 at Q4_0. Multiplied by recalled file sizes, that is about 311, 288 and 234 GB/s of a nominal 400, so the bus is used less as bits drop. The 24-core part decodes F16 at nearly the same speed, which means F16 is bound by memory bandwidth, but Q4_0 gains 12 percent from 33 percent more cores, so 4-bit decode is already partly compute-bound. That overturned the lens's own starting belief that there is about tenfold idle compute per weight byte at batch 1. The conclusion is that 'fewer bits is faster' stops being true near 4 bits on GPUs without matrix units unless the format costs no extra operations to decode. Diffusion sampling, LoRA training and LLM prefill are the opposite regime, compute-bound by a factor of hundreds, so only fewer multiply-adds help there. The lens also questioned whether the transformer body is where a small model's bytes go, estimating that the output head is roughly 20 to 60 percent of the weights below 1B parameters, and doubted that worst-case certified skipping survives at dimensions of 1024 and above.

### A timing deficit that may be a temperature artefact

*Lens: speech-and-audio-as-modelling-problems*

An October 2026 paper found two PersonaPlex instances hand over the floor at a median of 400 to 560 ms, where humans manage 137 ms, with only 1% of transfers in the last 120 ms of the partner's turn. The authors read this as reaction instead of projection. But they sampled the text stream at temperature 0.7, and starting to speak is a rare event per 80 ms frame. A rare event sampled below temperature one gets rarer. The lens ran the arithmetic: a model with exactly human timing, sampled at 0.7 with start probability spread over five to twenty possible first words, gives a median of 400 to 560 ms and 1 to 2% in the last 120 ms. Those are the paper's numbers. The lens says the match is close enough that it distrusts it a little. The broader pattern is that speech models make two kinds of decision with one sampler: what to say and when. When-decisions are nearly always small probabilities repeated over many frames: starting to speak, ending a pause, releasing a vowel, emitting a word in a streaming recogniser. Any sharpening chosen for the sake of what (low temperature, top-k, guidance) systematically delays or suppresses when. Text models never had to care, because a text token has no duration. The lens suspects much of the stiffness of machine speech comes from this one interaction, but could not write a single law because each model spreads event probability over different tokens.

## Added 2026-11-10, from the lab's first runs

- **Prefix-cache path noise on MPS bf16.** In case 002, scoring with a reused prefix cache moved low-probability tokens by tenths of a nat (68 of 312 re-scored comparisons differed, up to 0.438 nats; the gaps sat near log p of about −3.8). Confident tokens barely moved. Regressing the gap against p from the records already on disk would cost no device time. It touches "Token divergence follows a flip law" and the parked paired-metric-floor card.
- **The CPU matched the GPU for batch-1 decode on a tiny model.** The lab's Q003 found a CPU path scoring 0.342 against an MPS path's 0.340 of the reference time, with a tighter spread, limited by weight traffic. Where the crossover falls by model width, and whether a CPU draft model could run beside a GPU target in unified memory, are open; heterogeneous speculative decoding on phone chips may already be published.
- **Wrong answers that look like wrong lookups.** In case 002's first template the hundreds digit was nearly certain and the lower digits were often worse than a uniform guess, with answers off by about 20. If the wrong answers are sums of other dictionary values, retrieval, not arithmetic, failed.
- **Agent-run research fails in a recognisable way.** Every review layer here checked the instrument and missed the subject; cold reads by fresh agents found ten or more open decisions in every top card; a different-family reviewer caught a wrong bound and a broken identification; and the largest effect in the first run (the role-swap arm in one template) went unremarked by every reviewer, because each read only the planned contrasts. A table of defect class against the layer that caught it, built from the cards' review records and the lab's, would cost about an hour of reading. Two 2026 papers study failures of agent-run research qualitatively (arXiv 2601.03315; 2607.27191, seen as an abstract).

## Beliefs the lenses questioned

- **The obvious explanation of an anomaly is the right one.** (anomalies). In diffusers issue 14858 the first explanation was wrong and aspect ratio was the cause.
- **Equal validation loss means an equal model.** (backwards). Speedrun records now reach the target loss through lookups and caches, so the number can be gamed. It may have been misleading before that too, and nobody measured anything else.
- **A model that starts at a given loss is as far along as one that trained to it.** (backwards). Head starts from closed-form statistics might fade during training. People report the early gain and rarely check the end.
- **Whisper's padding frames are nothing.** (backwards). Frames after speech attend back to the speech, so the pad may be either constant and removable or used as memory.
- **Prompt word order does not matter, and text conditioning strength is the same at every resolution.** (backwards). Left-to-right LLM encoders cannot see later words. In joint attention the image side grows with resolution while text does not.
- **Every layer needs a gradient carried back through all the layers above it.** (build-something-new). Middle layers already live roughly in the output basis, so the transposed output matrix might serve as feedback.
- **Speech recognition needs amplitude.** (build-something-new). Old listening experiments, recalled but not opened, found sign-clipped speech stays understandable.
- **Long-horizon failure is mainly about running out of context length or memory.** (capabilities-agents-and-long-horizon-behaviour). Reported results show similar rival items, not length, drive retrieval failure, and agent loops keep producing such rivals.
- **Models that survive the start of a long task become safer as it goes on.** (capabilities-agents-and-long-horizon-behaviour). Mixing easy and hard tasks produces a falling population hazard by itself. Within a run, errors may compound instead.
- **Weight formats must be fixed-rate.** (compression). On Apple GPUs the multiply is fp16 regardless, so the decode is already paid and the CUDA tile rule does not apply.
- **Plain SGD at batch size 1 is as good as Adam.** (contradictions). The matching is on average loss. Rare-token progress was never reported in that setting and may be where SGD fails.
- **Fixed grids like the 10 ms mel hop and RoPE's 1 radian top band are harmless defaults.** (control-signal). Sampling theory says the hop folds pitch beats into syllable-rate wobble and RoPE leaves its top octaves empty, capping positional sharpness.
- **Damage from rounding or noise shows up as a small shift in averages.** (cross-pollination). Rare discrete decisions like stopping or detecting no speech have low base rates and react asymmetrically to noise, so they may break first.
- **A model compression study concerns one trained file.** (cross-pollination). The surprising 2026 rounding effects concern relations between files, such as unlearning that returns after 4-bit rounding.
- **Quantization error in a sampler is noise.** (crossmodal). It may be a per-sample bias that repeats each step, or partly a calibration shift, which calls for a different fix.
- **A sampler setting like 'T=0.8, top_p=0.95' identifies an experiment.** (data-and-tokens). Engines apply temperature and truncation in different orders, which changes entropy and candidate counts greatly.
- **Data methods can be judged by leaderboard gains alone.** (data-as-the-subject). Without stated floors and ceilings nobody knows whether a method is near its limit or the direction is worth little.
- **Curation by keep or discard teaches a model what to keep.** (data-as-the-subject). The decisions carry only a few hundred bytes, so most of what is learned is self-distillation, and in high-pass pipelines the discarded failures hold most of the bits.
- **Numerical divergence in token generation is chaos.** (dynamical-systems). Published bf16 and fp16 divergence points fit a flip law linear in precision, not the logarithmic law of chaos.
- **Temperature zero makes runs repeatable.** (dynamical-systems). Near-ties between top logits may make greedy flip rates comparable to shared-noise sampling, so the gain in repeatability may be small.
- **Community benchmarks of quantization, samplers and KV-cache settings establish which settings matter.** (folklore). Nearly every number is one seed, one model, context 512, so no one knows which effects exceed the noise.
- **Rounding a model's weights acts like raising the sampling temperature.** (folklore). Rounding error is fixed in time while temperature noise is fresh; higher temperature reduces loops, but rounding may raise them.
- **Picking the best stage-one model by its loss picks the best model for the next stage.** (future-work). Four unrelated papers find pretraining loss, initialisation quality or diffusion loss fails to predict fine-tuning, refinement or FID.
- **Recursive small networks help through extra computation at inference.** (future-work). Most of the score arrives at the first recursion step, and repeated supervision mattered more than hierarchy, hinting the benefit comes during training.
- **A pretrained encoder is needed for good diffusion latents.** (genmodel). JiT says no, and PixelREPA says alignment can fail for JiT.
- **Perceptual quality of generated media can be scored on short clips by crowds, and metrics correlated with humans are meaningful at small differences.** (human-in-loop). Complaints arise over long exposure as the listener learns the generator, and no just-noticeable-difference scale exists for any generative metric.
- **WER and naturalness are the right scores for speech tools.** (humanscale). Invented text is the real harm in captions, and expert blind listeners want very fast, deterministic speech rather than natural speech.
- **Temperature is a well-defined sampling knob.** (inference-algos). In continuous-token autoregressive models each paper defines it differently and none derives it.
- **Fisher information, posterior width and Cramer-Rao describe language model training error.** (information-geometry). Learning curves fall as power laws, not 1/n, so excess loss may be unfinished optimisation rather than estimation variance.
- **Classifier-free guidance is purely a sharpening trick unrelated to likelihood.** (information-geometry). Nobody measured the squared-error-optimal guidance weight; if above 1, part of guidance repairs under-use of the caption.
- **DiT models use the rich contextual information in LLM text embeddings.** (interp). 2606.03715 reports they mostly need only word meaning and word order.
- **Reward design determines how fast a rare exploit is found in RL.** (learning-rules-beyond-pretraining). A toy shows Adam changes the takeover-time exponent from about 1 to about 0.5, so the optimizer may set it.
- **RL forgetting less, tiny RL adapters, ES robustness and merge failures are separate phenomena.** (learning-rules-beyond-pretraining). They may all reflect a split of each update into a small signal part and a rule-dependent diffusion part.
- **Seed variance is one cause and harmless.** (mac-as-instrument). Harmless on average for loss is not harmless for timing of jumps or specific behaviours, and the seed can be split into init, data order and rounding.
- **A dense model spends the same energy on every token.** (mac-as-instrument). Matrix product power varies up to 38% with values on an A100, so energy may depend on content and unit ordering.
- **Distance from the full-precision or full-step output measures quality lost.** (measurement). Nobody publishes the noise floor of that distance, and one paper shows INT8 differs from FP8 by LPIPS 0.243 while preference metrics cannot tell them apart. No matched-noise control is subtracted.
- **The judge is independent of the system being judged.** (measurement). Caching is scored against the trajectory it copies, and TTS trained on Whisper transcripts is scored by Whisper.
- **Visual token pruning results measure the quality of the pruning method.** (multimodal-understanding-and-binding). Benchmarks may only need what a short caption carries, so pruning tolerance may measure the benchmark.
- **Weight decay gently and gradually penalises rare features.** (neuro-bio). Under AdamW the balance becomes a cliff at a frequency set by lambda and beta2, and it only appears in runs long enough to reach equilibrium.
- **Robustness is one number, usually flatness.** (neuro-bio). As in Marder's crabs, tolerance to rounding, activation noise and gain change may be separate traits.
- **Early layers are the fragile ones.** (neuro-bio). Residual norm growth acts as a restoring organ, which predicts noise damage should not depend on depth.
- **Layer and data attribution effects are roughly additive, and distant layers do not interact.** (no-boss). The premise is untested against a full lattice, which is cheap at 16 blocks, and RYS hints at strong local interactions.
- **Single-draw studies (one seed, one sample, a few hundred subsets) are adequate evidence.** (no-boss). Rare failures and minority mechanisms need thousands of samples; a ten-seed study misses a five-percent mechanism sixty percent of the time.
- **bfloat16 is effectively lossless for residual streams and optimizer state.** (numerical-analysis). Recursive sums with small terms accumulate error with partial sums; the residual stream may lose as much per block as 4-bit weight rounding, and Adam's second moment may never decay.
- **Same-seed LPIPS and FID are adequate ways to judge approximate samplers.** (numerical-analysis). They measure forward error; a backward-error Gaussianity test on implied noise could detect shifts with a hundred images and say which frequencies are wrong.
- **Guidance schedules are naturally expressed in sigma.** (numerical-analysis). Euler stability for flow matching depends on steps remaining, so any guidance instability would live in step-index units.
- **Each approximate-inference trick needs its own separate calibration.** (old-tricks). All of them inject noise into a deep circuit, so one per-model noise margin might predict tolerance to all of them.
- **Repetition loops in greedy decoding are a training or data problem.** (old-tricks). They may be quantizer limit cycles that need the argmax in the loop, which temperature cures as dither.
- **Long-form TTS failure is a length-generalization problem.** (old-tricks). It may be cycle slips in a tracking loop, with a flat rather than rising hazard.
- **Cross-hardware reproducibility needs a fixed summation order.** (outsider). Exact integer arithmetic makes summation order irrelevant.
- **The GPU is the only matrix engine in a Mac.** (outsider). The M1 Max CPU delivered 1.64 to 1.88 TFLOPS fp32 through Accelerate under load, against about 9 on the GPU.
- **Fewer bits means faster inference.** (physics). Measured M1 Max numbers show 4-bit decode is already partly compute-bound, so below about 4 bits a format only helps if it costs no extra decode operations. On M1 to M4, lower bits buy memory, not speed.
- **There is about tenfold idle compute per weight byte at batch 1.** (physics). True on paper from peak TFLOPS, but not in the measured kernel.
- **The transformer body is where a small model's bytes go.** (physics). By the lens's arithmetic the output head is roughly 20 to 60 percent of weights below 1B parameters.
- **Perplexity or Shannon entropy measures what a model can deliver.** (rate-distortion). Under search, the Renyi-1/2 entropy governs cost, and it can be far larger, so a model can know things no feasible search can extract.
- **Diffusion redundancy lives on the timestep axis and caching has nothing to skip at 4 to 8 steps.** (redundancy). Several 2026 papers claim gains at few steps, and other axes such as spatial frequency, activation rank and the VAE decoder are barely explored.
- **ASR-based checks are an adequate grade for speech models.** (robs-instruments). A voice-activity filter erased 306 of 315 words on breathy audio and Rob heard quality loss that ASR passed, so grading may be deaf to whole registers of voice.
- **Unwanted model habits can be removed by projecting out a direction.** (robs-instruments). Rob's catalog argues composition habits are absences, and an absence cannot be projected out.
- **Quantization error larger than a LoRA's contribution destroys the LoRA.** (robs-results). Base weights can act as dither that carries the update through rounding, up to a signal-to-noise threshold below which weak adapters vanish silently.
- **W4A4 results transfer to weight-only quantization, and outliers explain quantization damage.** (smallscience). Consumer hardware uses weight-only fp16, a different setting, and 'Beyond Outliers' shows outliers and kurtosis do not predict damage.
- **Duplex speech models react rather than project turn ends.** (speech-and-audio-as-modelling-problems). Sampling a rare per-frame start event at temperature 0.7 reproduces the reported delays from a model with exactly human timing.
- **Default guidance settings in flow-matching TTS are neutral.** (speech-and-audio-as-modelling-problems). Guidance may be an unlabelled clear-speech dial pushed to the lecture-hall end by word error rate, which is why the models sound like they are reading.
- **Softmax retrieval failure length is set by the logit range or gap.** (statistical-physics). The variance of distractor logits enters too; the variance-free bound is off by s squared over two.
- **Persona drift, repetition loops and prosody flattening are separate phenomena.** (tiny-model-science). An exact Bayesian self-conditioning urn has bounded drift, so all three may come from finite windows and sub-unit temperature turning the urn rich-get-richer.
- **Hallucination and sycophancy can be studied verbally on large checkpoints.** (tiny-model-science). Without a known Bayes-optimal answer, nobody can say how much deference is too much; toy worlds can measure it in nats.
- **A trained model is the object to study.** (year-2031). It is one draw from a seed distribution; much behaviour is improvised and fixed by early accidents, so agreement across seeds may be the real measure of knowledge.
- **Small RL or post-training updates teach reasoning.** (year-2031). Updates of 13 parameters or 1,023 weights suggest a switch over existing capability, possibly mostly formatting and length that a prompt could do.

## Ideas that died, and how

"Memory only" means the lens dropped the idea on what it remembered of prior work, without opening a paper. Those are the ones most worth a second look.

- **Long-video failure explained by token count rather than frame index.** (anomalies; memory only). UltraViCo and FreeLOC already cover it; these were seen through a search summary, not read.
- **Several assumptions to reverse: same seed means nothing across image models; learning-rate decay gain is the model learning more; ensembling is a poor trade against longer training; a fixed sampling seed is as good as any; separator tokens are ordinary; ViTs use all patch tokens for content; diffusion needs the noise level; duplicates only hurt; a tokenizer's canonical split is the only one understood.** (backwards; memory only). Already turned over by the field.
